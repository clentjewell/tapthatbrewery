**Execution capacity** is a real constraint – one marketer at about 70% cannot run segment campaigns, commercial outreach, automation, UGC and events at once. Two constraints sit above it.

**The business is losing money, and nobody has priced what it costs to run.** There is no COGS, no figure for carrying 27 taps, and no number for the taproom's monthly loss (open item #17). So the sequence below is not just about what one person can do. It is about what a business in this position can afford to attempt, and every priority that spends before the cheap acquisition is proven is deferred on those grounds.

**The order of the moves.** Ranked first: buy the system-owner databases, do a direct Harvey Norman deal, and go properly at the tour operators, commercial and wholesale. Ranked *below* those: pulling people into the taproom through social media. It is the obvious first move and it is the wrong one, because the taproom is the half that loses money.

Ranked; each states why now, what changes, and what we stop doing.

## P1 – Refill acquisition, switchers first

**Why now.** 116 of 206 owners (56%) bought their hardware elsewhere and switched with zero targeted effort – the cheapest acquisition available (no $975 hardware giveaway required, no 3-month buying cycle). Nobody on the Gold Coast owns the installed base of existing keg systems; Aardvark & Arrow* and Laughing Lizard sell kegs, not the relationship.
**What changes.** Two things.

**Buy the lists.** Harvey Norman sells six kegerator models plus a 25L tap unit. Keg Land / Kegmaster, BenchTop and Benchy hold buyer lists of their own. Every name on them owns a system and has already overcome the $975 objection we spend most of the budget fighting. At roughly $70 gross per keg the data pays for itself quickly, and this is the cheapest acquisition available to the business. Alongside it, a **direct arrangement with Harvey Norman** – point-of-sale QR, a referral rebate per keg, or a free keg with purchase where liquor rules allow. Monk holds point contacts there. This is a conversation, not a campaign.

**Then the always-on lane.** A standing "we fill any system" presence: dedicated landing page, Meta targeting of keg-owner behaviours, referral hook (500 Tap Tokens per refill referral already exists on the wall – put it online). First-fill offer priced against the $85–90 local kegs without discounting the range (H1 test).
**What we stop.** Treating every new active as someone who must first be sold a system.

## P2 – Lifecycle automation before new-audience spend

**Why now.** Churn management is a hand-sent monthly SMS ($20-off/2-week window). With active <75 days / at-risk 75–90 / churned 90+ already defined, and most churned customers "lapsed, not lost," the fix is mechanical – but blocked on GoTab/Fishbowl integration. Every month of delay leaks refill revenue from a list the business already owns.
**What changes.** Push the integration to done. Then model each customer's own reorder rhythm rather than running a calendar: people are creatures of habit and go through kegs at roughly their own rate, so a blanket 90-day message arrives at the wrong time for almost everyone. Arrive just ahead of the individual pattern – *"how's your keg looking?"* – with giveaway-entrant nurture matched to the three-month cycle (H3) and winback offers (including the Marie's Pizza/Buttercard test) triggered rather than remembered.

**Where the growth actually is.** Roughly 10% of customers buy monthly, 60% about every three months, and 40% run longer. The instinct is to work the 10%; they are already at ceiling and cannot drink much more. The 40% have **cut back rather than defected** – the system is still in the backyard. Reactivating them needs a product, not a louder message: a low-alcohol or healthier line, framed as hydration rather than beer. Where those customers are also members, they are paying for something they have stopped using, and that is the most winnable revenue in the set.
**What we stop.** Manual monthly SMS as the retention system; spending on cold reach while the warm list leaks.

## P3 – Re-weight effort from taproom to engine

**Why now.** The last 18 months drifted to the venue. It loses money, and the tension underneath it is this: **the taproom and the refill model are zero-sum.** The refill business is built on people *not* coming in, so every campaign that fills the room fills the half that loses. The 20–30% visitor-to-owner conversion that justified treating it as a funnel is your own estimate and has never been measured (open item #18) – at that rate the rational move would be to pay people to walk in, which is itself a reason to doubt it.

This is why the taproom is a founders' decision (open item #20) rather than a marketing allocation: run it as a restricted-hours tasting and event venue, or keep paying for a drop-in. Until that is settled, the allocation below is an interim, not a strategy.
**What changes.** Taproom marketing is scoped to what feeds conversion: events that fill the room (ticketed nights already do a week’s trade in 5 hours), the demo moment, giveaway entry capture. Harry’s 70% marketing time is allocated engine-first (P1/P2) with a fixed, small taproom quota. Taproom→system conversion becomes a tracked number, not folklore.
**What we stop.** Venue-first content calendars; founder and marketer attention defaulting to whatever is visible and social.

## P4 – Commercial and wholesale, properly resourced

**This ranks near the top, and the arithmetic is the reason.** One commercial account is worth roughly **ten households**: lifetime gross profit runs about **$21,840** on a venue account against **$2,100** residential, and one event drives a week of taproom trade. Cold walk-ins failing says something about *method*, not about the size of the opportunity.

**Why now.** The earlier failure is real – cold walk-ins and emails to Broadbeach restaurants went nowhere, DM leads got responses but follow-up was loose, and NRL-affiliated clubs are CUB-locked. What that shows is that unowned, unstructured outreach fails. The answer is blunt: *"Employ people there rather than employ social media managers."*

**What changes.**
- **Structure, goals, KPIs and a named owner** – an FTE or an agent. Not a channel in a plan.
- **Tour operators, proactively.** Hop On, Urban Legends and Pineapple Tours all let the customer choose which breweries they visit, so the write-up decides whether Tap That gets picked. Ours is perfunctory and does not mention the award-winning Midnight in Tokyo while Balter gets the imagery. Fresh copy, photos, reviews and awards to each operator. Urban Legends makes customers book each tasting themselves; building them a booking tool that always includes Tap That is chargeable work in its own right.
- **Sports clubs as a distinct play.** Customise the system and the brew; place it free against a minimum guarantee of X kegs a month across the season; move the system from footy to cricket so it is not idle half the year. Occupancy is the metric.
- **Wholesale needs its WIIFM.** Margin, white label and cuisine matching are the arguments a venue buyer needs, and none of them are currently made anywhere. White-label ("Surfers Paradise Demons Lager", 10% player discount / 10% club kickback – H6) stays, but as one offer inside a proposition rather than the whole of it.

**What we stop.** Unowned prospecting with no follow-up cadence; chasing CUB-locked venues; and treating this as opportunism to be picked up when there is time.

## P5 – Collateral and pricing hygiene

**Why now.** Two live membership posters disagree ($250/yr vs $300 + $120/yr renewal, different token values); the tasting paddle is $18 on the sign and $20 on screen. Trivial in-venue; fatal online – no funnel, pricing page or membership pitch can be built on unreconciled numbers, and it erodes the "consistent quality" positioning that justifies $120–170 kegs against $85–90 rivals.
**What changes.** One price list, reconciled and dated, as the single source for all Design-phase copy; old poster removed. This now includes the per-schooner figure, where three sets of maths are in circulation – $2.70, $2.98/$2.34 and $2.55/$3.19, from three different keg prices and two schooner counts (open item #19). There is a live ad running on one of them.
**What we stop.** Publishing anything customer-facing before reconciliation – and specifically, correcting the live "$2.34 a schooner" claim by substituting another number. Pull it until #19 closes.

## P6 – Measure the engine, not the noise

**Why now.** The $2.6M target decomposes into one number – active keg customers – yet today’s evidenced measurement is a leaderboard photo and headline census figures. A team of Harry plus two founders cannot act on a dashboard they don’t have.
**What changes.** #26 Measurement Plan implemented: active count weekly, the supporting funnel monthly, sourced from GoTab/Fishbowl/Buffer. And the measurement that is missing entirely – **cost**. COGS, the true cost of carrying 27 taps, and the taproom's actual monthly loss. Without those, every priority above is a judgement about revenue with no view of margin, and we would rather say so than imply otherwise.
**What we stop.** Judging marketing by taproom busyness and follower counts; and presenting revenue plans as business cases while the cost side is unknown.

## Assumptions & open items

- Ranking assumes GoTab/Fishbowl integration is achievable near-term – timeline unconfirmed; if it slips past Stage 1, P2 tactics run semi-manually off exported lists.
- Switcher-pool size (existing GC keg owners) is inferred, not counted.
- *Aardvark and Arrow, confirmed 27 August. Domain suspended and merged with Burleigh Homebrew.
- P3 assumes founders accept a capped taproom remit – but the underlying taproom decision (open item #20) outranks it and should be settled first.
- P4's reversal rests on the venue-versus-household lifetime figures, which came through with the client's records and have not been independently checked.
- The sports-club and tour-operator plays are advisory-review proposals, not tested propositions.
- Ranking cannot be finalised without the cost data (#17). What is here is the best sequence on revenue logic alone.
