## 1. The core verdict on the Discovery output

Christy's headline: the Discovery pack plays the conversation back accurately, but it is not hard hitting enough for a client whose business is losing money.

> "It felt like it's almost a bit light in a way. It's not hard hitting, right? Because I tell you what I wrote down from the conversation that I didn't think Discover captured." (64:31)

> "That's where I think the Discover thing talks too much about marketing and the consumer, and it's a bit... for him he's desperate. He needs a sermon rather than 'hey, it's nice'." (133:25)

**Four things Discovery does not say, and must:**

1. **The business is loss making.** Not named anywhere in the output. (64:53)
2. **The tap room and the refill model are a zero sum game.** The refill business is built on people not coming in. So driving traffic to the tap room drives people into a loss making exercise. "If you're sitting on the beach in Burleigh, I get it. But you're not." (64:53)
3. **Cost drivers are missing entirely.** 27 flavours on keg means ingredients, kegs, storage and refrigeration on stock that may not move. "We haven't really captured the cost drivers to be able to say, here's how we're going to design the next phase business." (64:53)
4. **The commercial opportunity is buried.** One commercial account is worth ten household accounts. One event drives a week's worth of tap room business. Both are in the docs but not as headline points. (67:29)

**Two data problems to fix before Design:**

- **The 20 to 30 per cent tap room to refill conversion is not credible.** It recurs throughout the docs and Design is now using it as a proof point for the strategy. "If that's true, just pay people to come to the tap room. That's a huge conversion rate and I can't believe it." (76:00)
- **The 50 person census is too small and too self reported to anchor strategy on.** People do not admit the real reasons. "You're not going to say 'I want to be seen as more successful than my mates' out loud. You're going to go, it's all about the taste." Christy wants gut feel to lead, with the census as a secondary input, presented as "your census said this, our gut says this, what do you think?" (115:57)
- **The 1,000 entries on the system giveaway is a demand signal, but an untested one.** How many of those people actually want a system permanently in their backyard, and how many just want it free for an occasion and would roll it out of the garage twice a year? Nothing in Discovery interrogates it. Everybody enters a free competition. (87:20)

**Correction required:** the Discovery documents call Harry "Rafe" throughout. Harry is the marketing person, Justin is the owner. (80:10)

## 2. Website and brand critique

- Above the fold does nothing. Background video cuts too fast to read and does not show people having a good time.
- Too much copy on the opening page. Low resolution static logo.
- The Crafted award needs to be on the front page as a badge.
- "Fits any system, any fridge" needs to be immediate and obvious.
- Tap room reviews are buried at the bottom of the scroll. They should be a scrolling reviews block above the fold.
- If the strategy is refills, the site should feel like it is about refills. At present everything is weighted equally.
- Wholesale is under sold. The copy says "increase profitability" where it should say "you make great margin". Missing entirely: white labelling for restaurants, and cuisine matching (Japanese beer with a Japanese meal, Mexican with Mexican).
- Two membership tiers (keg refill vs tap room mug club) should be a single programme that rewards both behaviours. (70:54)
- Reference site Clent shared as the standard: **Drink Hopper**, including the scratch and reveal mystery discount that captures an email, and subscribe on autopilot. (118:04)

## 3. Growth ideas raised

**Databases and channel partnerships (the highest leverage items)**

- Harvey Norman sells kegerators, six listed on their site now plus a 25 litre unit. That is the single biggest traffic opportunity for refills. Options: buy or rent the South East Queensland buyer database, a point of sale QR code, a referral rebate on each keg, or a free keg with purchase if liquor rules allow. Monk is one of Harvey Norman's closest allies and has point contacts there. (72:53, 75:34)
- Same play for Keg Land / Kegmaster buyer databases, and the BenchTop ("benchy") system buyer database. (121:01, 99:21)
- At roughly 70 dollars of margin per keg, buying the data is worth the investment.

**Brewery tours**

- Three or four operators on the Coast: Hop On, Urban Legends, Pineapple Tours. Most ask the customer to pick which breweries to visit, so the write up decides whether you get chosen.
- Tap That's write up is perfunctory. No mention of the award winning Midnight in Tokyo lager. Balter gets the imagery, Tap That does not. Harry should proactively send new copy, photos, reviews and awards to each operator. (78:26)
- Urban Legends makes customers book every tasting themselves, then drives them around. Build them an online tour booking widget that sits on their site and always includes, or prioritises, Tap That. Christy flagged this as genuinely chargeable work; Clent reckons Ronnie could build it inside 24 hours off a brief. (80:49, 128:18)

**Commercial and wholesale**

- Needs structure, goals and KPIs, and a dedicated person or agent carrying the product. Target pubs, restaurants and sports clubs. "Employ people there rather than employ social media managers." (115:23, 133:25)

**Product and pricing**

- Cut to about six core beers plus seasonal specials. 27 creates decision anxiety, an unreadable board and no navigation. "It feels like the manufacturer is running the business." (137:11, 138:08)
- Sell the unit economics, not the keg price: 2.70 dollars a schooner against 10 to 18 dollars at the pub. The enemy is the pub and the bottle shop, not the competitor across the road.
- Tap That sits at 120 dollars a keg for members, 150 for non members, up to about 300 at the top end. The operator across the road is 85 to 95 and has just had to merge, website currently down. Being more expensive is defensible if the story is unit economics.
- This is the reverse razor and blades model: the system is expensive and the refills are cheap. Fix it with rental or lease to buy, framed like a gym membership, 50 to 100 dollars a month, to remove the upfront barrier. (91:05)
- Free delivery, especially as a member benefit. Or 10 dollars for one keg, free for two. Gets Justin out in front of customers talking about beer instead of standing at the bar waiting. Add a free two litre growler of a seasonal as surprise and delight. (108:41)
- **Second Earth Brewery as the counter example on positioning.** They brought an investor in who told them they did not know their arsehole from their elbow, because they sat in the middle ground, neither cheap nor expensive. They put a dark beer into 500ml cans at about 50 dollars for four and now cannot keep up with demand. Clent's point: a canner is not expensive, and premium beats middle. (111:13)
- Collaborations with barbecue brands: an all in one backyard barbecue plus four tap unit, the Rolls Royce of the backyard (Backyard Systems, Bernabie style four tap systems). Easier to sell into the household because it goes to the partner as a barbecue, not a beer rig.
- The RTD range is an afterthought. Women are treated as covered rather than catered for. "This is a bloke's beer place and the chicks can come if they want. But the blokes will follow the girls." Clent agreed this is a real blind spot for Justin. (100:14)
- Health and lifestyle trend is running against volume beer: carb free, protein enhanced, remove the guilt factor. The demographic is ageing. Sell quality of connection over quantity. Clent put it bluntly: "You and I are squarely in the target market, and we are both sitting here knowing that a keg refill business based on volume is not right." (89:47)
- **A healthy or low alcohol line is a distinct product recommendation, not just a trend note.** The lapsed members still have the system in the backyard and still need to hydrate, they have just cut back on beer. Put something they will drink into their taps and you reactivate them without asking them to drink more. (113:25)

**CRM**

- The 90 day blanket SMS is tone deaf. Model actual purchase patterns and reach out ahead of the reorder. "Hey Kevin, it's about time. How's your keg looking?"
- The numbers: 10 per cent buy monthly, 60 per cent every three months, 40 per cent longer than that. Census says the long tail have either cut back or did not get around to it. **That 40 per cent is where the growth is**, not the monthly buyers who cannot drink much more. (113:25)
- Simplify loyalty. Every tenth keg free beats a points system whose administration may not be worth the squeeze, and the points tracking is itself a cost driver.
- Pay with a tweet, and discounts for tagging Tap That in user generated content.

## 4. The tap room: stop running it as a drop in

Christy's strongest structural recommendation.

> "Microbrew needs to be the destination, not the journey." (83:08)

- Restrict hours and run it as a tasting room and event venue, for example Saturday 5 to 8, rather than sitting open waiting for people.
- It needs an X factor. Shuffleboard is not it. Candidates raised: axe throwing, a smash or destruction room (Smash Room Brisbane as a pop up partner), a motorbike wheelie machine (there is a motorbike shop next door), pour your own tap tables or booths, a spectacular serving vessel rather than a 20 litre keg.
- Let members keep their own keg at the tap room, the way some pubs hold your whiskey bottle. Needs a business model attached since they drink at keg price, so the money has to come from what else they buy. Clent's variant was "have your own bin".
- Content that writes itself once the venue has a format: man versus keg, beer versus food, and pour your own footage for user generated content.
- Weddings and the wedding trail. Clent has two or three events contacts to introduce.
- The location is the problem that cannot be fixed. An industrial park cannot compete for organic foot traffic. Christy's benchmark was a venue ten minutes away with karaoke, free pool, live sport and, above all, an owner and staff who create the atmosphere.

> "You've got to decide whether you're beer nerds, which is a business, or hospitality people. Hospitality people come from hospitality, and location is the most important thing, not an industrial park." (105:18)

- Both Clent and Christy noted Justin is sharp but not naturally hospitable, and the socials do not carry any energy or a face.
- **Chris, the head brewer, is invisible.** Apparently well regarded, but Clent has been in many Friday nights and has never once had the tasting notes explained to him. Profile him, get him front and centre talking about beer. (110:42)
- Harry and Justin brainstorming social content is "fiddling while Rome burns". Harry needs stronger, narrower KPIs and Justin needs to direct him. (119:57)

## 6. Process and commercial conclusions

**On the 3D process**

- It suits a greenfield client with an idea, not a rescue of an existing struggling business. "This is coming in and pulling apart someone's mess."
- For Tap That, Discovery is a smart input to the next step, not the output. (136:52)
- The front end questions were wrong. Design cannot fix a business plan when Discovery only asked marketing questions.
- Clent: "We're not just sales and marketing. There's a business order in here and we need to go back and get the actual correct questions at the front end." (135:15)

**On speed**

- Clent named the pattern: too much building, micro focus, failure to deliver in a timely way.
- Target model going forward: a 20 minute initial conversation, not two hours, then a fast, automated turnaround of something valuable. "We'll spend an hour of our time, not a day each." (125:48)
- Christy: if the USP is "we can turn this around this afternoon", you cannot come back a week and a half later saying "we heard you".

**On charging**

- Roughly two days of effort already in, call it five grand of value, delivered risk free. Clent's view is there should be a premium on what comes next because they carried the risk.
- Christy's view: do not try to make money on this one. Charge for execution, where the cost and the deliverable are tangible. Named candidates: PR and tour operator outreach, the Harvey Norman approach, and building the Urban Legends booking widget.
- Structure it as: here are the ten recommendations, Harry takes these, we will do these for you at a cost. (124:37)

**The prioritisation call, in Clent's own framing**

Clent put the "Shag, Marry, Push off a Cliff" question to Christy: of everything raised, which three do we actually recommend? Christy's three were:

1. Chase the databases: Harvey Norman, Keg Land / Kegmaster, BenchTop, anyone who has sold a system.
2. Do a deal with Harvey Norman directly, using Monk's contacts.
3. Go proactively at the tour companies, and go hard at commercial and wholesale.

Explicitly ranked below those: trying to pull people into the tap room through social media. (120:49)

**On the next deliverable**

- A one on one deck, around 10 to 12 slides, presented to Justin and Harry, with the detail sitting behind it.
- The reciprocity is already set up: they came out twice, sat him down, worked on his business. That earns the right to ask him to go back to his partner or investor for budget.

**Resourcing note:** Raef's Global Health contract finishes this week and he has a couple of months free (less two weeks in Japan). Clent wants that time spent stripping Maxxim back to a core product and pushing it out.
