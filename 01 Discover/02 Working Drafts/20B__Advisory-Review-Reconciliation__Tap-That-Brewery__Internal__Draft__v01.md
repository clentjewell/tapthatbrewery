## Why this document exists

On 27 August the set went to Christy Kilmartin for review before anything reached the client. The verdict was not that the work is wrong. It is that the work is **not hard enough for a business that is losing money**.

> "It felt like it's almost a bit light in a way. It's not hard hitting... he's desperate. He needs a sermon rather than 'hey, it's nice'."

That is a fair hit and it is recorded here rather than absorbed quietly. Three things follow: some of what we wrote is now unsupported, some of what we omitted is the most important material in the engagement, and the sequencing of the whole engagement changes.

**Source precedence.** Christy's typed notes are the primary record; the transcript is secondary. Where the notes and the session differ in emphasis, the notes win.

## 4. What the review adds

Ranked as Christy ranked them. The first three are the recommendation; everything else sits below.

### The three that matter

1. **Buy the databases.** Harvey Norman sells six kegerator models plus a 25L tap unit. Keg Land / Kegmaster and BenchTop hold buyer lists too. Every name on them owns a system and has already overcome the objection we spend most of our budget fighting. At ~$70 gross per keg, buying the data pays for itself quickly.
2. **Do a deal with Harvey Norman directly.** Point-of-sale QR, a referral rebate per keg, or a free keg with purchase if liquor rules allow. Monk has point contacts there.
3. **Go proactively at the tour operators, and go hard at commercial and wholesale.** Hop On, Urban Legends and Pineapple Tours all let the customer pick the breweries, so the write-up decides whether Tap That gets chosen — and ours is perfunctory, with no mention of the award-winning Midnight in Tokyo. Wholesale needs structure, goals, KPIs and a dedicated person. *"Employ people there rather than employ social media managers."*

**Explicitly ranked below those: pulling people into the taproom through social media.** That is close to the centre of our current Deploy plan.

### Product and pricing

- **Cut to about six core beers plus seasonals.** Twenty-seven creates decision anxiety and an unreadable board. *"It feels like the manufacturer is running the business."*
- **Sell the unit economics, not the keg price.** A schooner at home against $10–18 at the pub. The enemy is the pub and the bottle shop, not the operator across the road.
- **This is the reverse razor-and-blades model** — expensive handle, cheap blades. Fix it with rental or lease-to-buy framed as a gym membership, $50–100/month, to remove the upfront barrier.
- **Free delivery as a member benefit**, or $150 non-member / $130 member delivered — share the cost, make the margin back in dollars rather than percentage. It also gets Justin in front of customers instead of behind the bar.
- **A healthy or low-alcohol line is a product recommendation, not a trend note.** Lapsed members still own the system; they have cut back on beer, not on thirst. Put something they will drink into their taps and you reactivate them without asking anyone to drink more.
- **The RTD range is an afterthought and women are covered rather than catered for.** *"This is a bloke's beer place and the chicks can come if they want. But the blokes will follow the girls."*
- **Positioning counter-example — Second Earth Brewery.** They sat in the middle ground, neither cheap nor premium, until an investor told them so. They put a dark beer into 500ml cans at ~$50 for four and now cannot keep up. Premium beats middle.

### CRM and loyalty

- **The 90-day blanket SMS is tone-deaf.** Model actual purchase patterns and reach out ahead of the reorder: *"Hey Kevin, it's about time. How's your keg looking?"*
- **10% buy monthly, 60% every three months, 40% longer.** The 40% is where the growth is — not the monthly buyers, who cannot drink much more. Especially important where they are members.
- **Simplify loyalty.** Every tenth keg free beats a points system whose administration is itself a cost driver.
- **Affiliate:** a free keg for both parties when a mate buys a system. Pay-with-a-tweet and discounts for tagging.

### The taproom

> "Microbrew needs to be the destination, not the journey."

- **Stop running it as a drop-in.** Restrict hours; run it as a tasting room and event venue — Saturday 5–8 rather than sitting open waiting.
- **It needs an X factor.** Shuffleboard is not it. Axe throwing, a smash room, a motorbike wheelie machine, pour-your-own tables, or a spectacular serving vessel.
- **The puppy-dog close:** put a system in a booth so customers use one before they buy. Let members keep their own keg the way a pub holds your whiskey bottle.
- **Chris is invisible.** The head brewer is well regarded and has never once explained the tasting notes to a Friday-night regular. Profile him.
- **The location cannot be fixed.** An industrial park will not win organic foot traffic. *"You've got to decide whether you're beer nerds, which is a business, or hospitality people."*

### Website and brand

Nothing above the fold works: background film cut too fast to read, too much copy, a low-resolution static logo, four business units given equal weight. The Crafted award and "fits any system" both belong on the landing page. Reviews are buried behind click-dots. Wholesale is missing its WIIFM entirely — margin, white label, cuisine matching. One membership should span taproom and refill. Reference standard: **Drink Hopper**.

## 7. What is still missing

- **Cost data.** COGS, the true cost of 27 taps, the taproom's actual monthly loss. Without it, no business recommendation is more than an opinion.
- **A measured taproom conversion rate.** The 20–30% figure needs replacing with something observed.
- **The Tuesday site-visit session** with Justin and Harry is not in the reviewed material.
- **The unit-economics claim needs reconciling.** Christy quotes $2.70 a schooner; our documents say $2.98 non-member and $2.34 member. On the verified price list — $120 member, $150 non-member for a core 20L — 47 schooners gives **$2.55 member and $3.19 non-member**. Three different figures are in circulation for the single most quotable number in the business. Settle it before it goes in an ad.
