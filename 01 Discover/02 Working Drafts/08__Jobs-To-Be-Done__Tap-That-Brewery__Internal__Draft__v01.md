## Frame

Nobody hires a keg system for the hardware – the client said it themselves: *"we’re in the business of connection and good times – keg systems and refills are the means, not the end."* This document states what each segment is actually hiring Tap That Brewery to do, in JTBD form (*When [situation], help me [job] so that [outcome]*), split functional / social / emotional. It then treats the four purchase moments – system, refill, taproom visit, membership – as **distinct jobs**, because a customer can fire Tap That Brewery from one while still employing it for another.

## Jobs by segment

| Segment | Functional job | Social job | Emotional job |
|---|---|---|---|
| Young families | When going out means babysitters and $300 nights, help me bring the good night home so the social life survives the kids. | When my mates come over, help me host without effort so my place stays the default. | Help me feel like having kids didn’t end the fun – it just moved the venue. |
| High-volume drinkers | When my weekly bottle-shop run costs $100+, help me get better beer for less so the habit stops stinging. (44 schooners per 20L keg, $120–170.) | When I shout the boys, help me pour like a publican instead of handing out tins. | Help me feel smart about what I drink – fresher, cheaper, sorted – not guilty about it. |
| Cashed-up tradies | When the shed build is nearly done, help me finish it with the piece everyone talks about. | When mates see the setup, help me be the bloke whose shed has beer on tap. | Help me feel the build – and the money – was worth it. |
| Baby boomers | When the family descends on weekends, help me keep everyone poured without playing waiter. | When we host, help me entertain generously – proper beer for the boys, seltzer for the granddaughters. | Help me feel like the hub the family gathers around. |
| Share houses | When four of us split everything, help us own the party asset no other house has. | Help our place be the one people show up to. | Help us feel like the $975 four ways was the easiest money we ever spent. |
| DINKs | When we’d rather two great drinks than a fridge of average ones, help us pour top-shelf and cocktails at home. | Help our place read as considered – the good stuff, on tap. | Help us feel taste is something we own, not order. |
| Workplaces | When Friday knock-offs deserve better than a car-park slab, help us make the office worth staying at. | Help the boss look like the boss. | Help the team feel looked after. |
| Partner / veto-holder | When he wants a $975 beer system, help me see what’s in it for the whole household. (~1/3 of 27 taps non-beer; gluten-reduced, lower-carb.) | When people are over, help me host well too – gin fizz and seltzer on tap, not just his lager. | Help me feel this was *our* good decision, not his toy. |
| Existing system owners | When my keg’s empty, help me get it filled fresh, fast and consistently – whoever sold me the hardware. | Help me be the owner whose system always pours well. | Help me feel the system was a good call after all. |

**The pattern:** every primary segment’s social job is a variant of *make my place the default venue*. That is the campaign-level job – the pillars (kudos, connection) are just its two faces, and cost is the justification that follows (Christy’s dirty-little-secret rule).

## Jobs by purchase moment

The four moments hire Tap That Brewery for different things. Conflating them produces creative that sells everything and closes nothing.

| Moment | The job being hired | Success looks like | Fired when |
|---|---|---|---|
| **System purchase** ($975–$2,550, at cost) | *Upgrade my home into the venue.* A considered household identity purchase – closer to a barbecue or spa than to beer | The build finished, the first pour in front of mates, the partner on side | The barriers win: upfront cost, veto, space, self-trust (see 07) |
| **Refill** (~$200/month; the margin engine) | *Never let the tap run dry, without thinking about it.* A convenience/habit job – logistics, not identity | Always something good pouring; the swap is effortless (plastic pick-up-and-go; delivery pending) | The errand outweighs the habit: 25-minute round trips, corny Tue–Fri wait, "haven’t gotten around to it" drift past 90 days |
| **Taproom visit** | *Show me the life before I buy it.* For locals: a good, cheap session (Knockoffs happy hour, $18–20 paddle). For prospects: the live demo | The "that’s a keg system?" moment – 20–30% [unverified – see #20B] of visitors become owners | It’s just another brewery visit: no demo beat, no follow-up capturing the 3-month window |
| **Membership** (Keg Crew $250/yr) | *Make my habit pay and make me a regular.* $30 off every refill (repays in ~4 months at ~1.55 kegs/month (census)), 100 Tap Tokens, 5L birthday keg | Belonging with maths behind it – leaderboard, tasting nights, mugs at schooner prices | The value is unclear (two conflicting posters) or unused (lapsed refill cadence makes the fee feel wasted) |

**The system linking them:** the taproom sells the *dream*, the system purchase buys the *identity*, refills are the *habit* the margin lives on, and membership is the *contract with the habit*. Each moment’s creative should do its own job – a refill SMS never needs to resell the dream; a system campaign should never lead with a refill discount.

## Assumptions & open items

- JTBD statements are Jewell constructions on client-stated segment logic and objections – pressure-test the wording against census verbatims and real customer conversations before they anchor Design-phase copy.
- Membership payback maths uses the newer poster ($250/yr, $30/refill) – pending the pricing reconciliation flagged in D06.
- The veto-holder jobs assume the partner is beer-averse; census may show a share who are direct customers of the non-beer taps in their own right – which would upgrade her from veto-holder to co-buyer in the messaging.
