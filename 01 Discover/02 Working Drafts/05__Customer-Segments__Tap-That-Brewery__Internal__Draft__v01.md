## What changed, and why it matters

The first draft of this model did one big thing: it took the discovery-session read – that cost is the customer's dirty little secret, with kudos and connection leading – and **inverted it** on the strength of the census ranking. Taste first, cost second, kudos last.

The advisory review put that inversion under question, and the challenge is a fair one:

> "Census = 50 people who don't really know why they do something, don't do what they say. Be careful placing too much emphasis on this for strategy. Ego. Want to be seen as (more) successful is a key driver that won't be said out loud."

So the census is now **one input, not the arbiter**. Fifty self-selected respondents can tell us what they will *admit* to; they cannot tell us what moves them. Taste ranks first partly because taste is the respectable answer. The original discovery read was not wrong – it was measuring something a survey cannot reach.

The review also found this model missing three things entirely: **cadence**, **commercial**, and a **partner segment catered to rather than managed**. Those are now axes in their own right, and two of them outrank the demographic lenses below.

## How to read this model

There are three axes, and they are not interchangeable.

| Axis | What it sorts by | Why it ranks here |
|---|---|---|
| **1 · Refill cadence** | How often an existing customer actually buys | The margin engine. Cheapest revenue in the business and the only axis measurable from your own data |
| **2 · Commercial and wholesale** | Account type, not person | One commercial account is worth roughly ten households. Ranked near the top of the advisory review's recommendations |
| **3 · Household lenses** | Demography and life stage | Still useful for creative direction. **Message lenses, not exclusive boxes** – they overlap deliberately and do not sum to 100% |

Pillars remain **Cost · Convenience · Connection · Kudos · Taste/Freshness**. The census ranks them 1 Taste & Freshness **2.19**, 2 Cost **2.75**, 3 Convenience **2.81**, 4 Connection **3.61**, 5 Kudos **3.64**. Treat that as *the order people will say out loud*. Ego, status and – bluntly – wanting a reason not to be in the house are unsaid drivers that no self-report will surface, and creative should be built knowing both.

One structural note that governs everything below. **The business is loss-making, and the taproom and the refill model work against each other**: refills are built on people *not* coming in. Any segment reached primarily by driving taproom traffic is being acquired through the half of the business that loses money. That does not make it wrong. It makes it a cost that has to be named.

## Axis 1 – Refill cadence (the margin engine)

Your own records give the shape: roughly **10% buy monthly, 60% about every three months, and 40% run longer than that**. The instinct is to work the 10%. The opportunity is the 40%.

| Band | Share | What is actually happening | The move |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Monthly** | ~10% | Already at ceiling. They cannot drink much more, and asking them to is the wrong pitch | Protect. Membership, priority, first access to seasonals |
| **Quarterly** | ~60% | The core. Habit is set but loose; the gap is where churn starts | Reach them **before** the reorder, not after |
| **Lapsed / longer** | ~40% | **They have cut back, not defected.** They still own the system. It is sitting there | The growth is here – and it needs a different product, not a louder ad |

**The 40% is the finding.** They have not left; they have moderated. If they are still showing connection and kudos drivers – still hosting, still wanting the tap wall to say something about them – then the answer is to put something in their taps they will actually drink. That means **a low-alcohol or healthier line, framed as hydration rather than beer**. It reactivates a customer who already owns the hardware without asking anyone to drink more, which is the only honest version of this pitch. The trend runs the same way: the health and lifestyle shift is against volume beer, and the customer base is ageing. The pitch to this band is **quality of connection over quantity consumed**. Where those customers are also Keg Crew members, the case is stronger again: they are paying for a membership they have stopped using.

**On the 90-day SMS.** People are creatures of habit and go through kegs at roughly their own rate. A blanket 90-day message is tone-deaf against that. Model each customer's actual pattern and arrive just ahead of it – *"how's your keg looking?"* – rather than on a calendar that suits us. This is a CRM build (#48, #58), not a copy change.

**Blocked on:** the cadence bands above come from client observation, not counted refills. Square holds the real distribution (open item #12), and what counts as "active" is still undecided at 45, 75 or 90 days (open item #11) – which is the difference between an active base of **96 and 113**.

## Axis 2 – Commercial, wholesale and clubs

The first draft put workplaces at ~5% and said none of the secondary segments warranted a campaign. On the advisory review's arithmetic that is the wrong call.

**One commercial account is worth roughly ten households. One event drives a week of taproom trade.** Both facts were already sitting in our documents; neither was a headline. They should be.

- **Venue and commercial accounts.** Lifetime gross profit on a venue account runs to about **$21,840** against roughly **$2,100** for a residential customer. That ratio is the argument for a dedicated person rather than a campaign. The review is blunt about it: *"Employ people there rather than employ social media managers."* Wholesale outreach needs structure, goals, KPIs and an owner – an FTE or an agent – not a channel plan.
- **Sports clubs.** A distinct segment with its own mechanic: customise the system and the brew, and place it free against a minimum guarantee of X kegs a month across the season. Then **move the system from footy to cricket** so it is not idle for half the year. Occupancy is the metric, not units sold.
- **Tour operators.** Hop On, Urban Legends and Pineapple Tours all let the customer choose which breweries they visit, which means the write-up decides whether Tap That gets picked. Ours is perfunctory and does not mention the award-winning Midnight in Tokyo. Urban Legends makes customers book each tasting themselves; building them a booking tool that always includes Tap That is chargeable work in its own right.
- **Wholesale is missing its WIIFM.** Margin, white label and cuisine matching are the arguments a venue buyer needs, and none of them are currently made anywhere.

## Axis 3 – Household lenses

Retained from the first draft, with the ranking intact and the unsaid drivers added. These are lenses for creative, not a media plan on their own.

| # | Lens | Share (est.) | Pillar emphasis | Said driver vs unsaid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Young families | ~45% | Connection (cost closes) | Says: hosting. Also true: a night out costs more than a keg, and the backyard is the cheaper social life |
| 2 | High-volume drinkers | ~20% | Cost + convenience | Says: convenience. Also true: the bottle-shop run is a chore and the maths is a permission slip |
| 3 | Cashed-up tradies | ~20% | Kudos + connection | Says: quality. Also true: the mates' reaction is the purchase. Status, plainly |
| 4 | Baby boomers | ~20% | Connection; taste/freshness | Says: quality entertaining. Genuinely means it – the lowest gap between stated and real |
| 5 | DINKs | ~5% | Kudos + taste | Premium range, cocktails on tap |
| 6 | Share houses | ~5% | Kudos + cost | $975 split four ways is trivial; houses dissolve and the address churns |
| 7 | Status buyers | ~5% | Kudos | Integrated systems from $1,750. The unsaid driver *is* the driver |

*Workplaces have been promoted out of this table into Axis 2.*

**Young families (~45%).** The Burleigh–Palm Beach–Miami–Tugun belt: mortgaged 30s–40s households where the backyard, not the venue strip, is the social unit. Babysitters plus a night out costs more than a keg, and the school-and-nippers network is the word-of-mouth channel. The purchase is a household decision – see the partner section below. Guardrail from #04: this must not become "the dad brand".

**High-volume drinkers (~20%).** The one lens where the maths can lead openly – a 20L keg is 44 schooners against a $100+ bottle-shop weekend. The per-schooner figure is a genuine selling point and it is **not settled**: $2.70, $2.98 and $3.19 all follow from different keg prices and schooner counts (open item #19), so no creative carries a number until it closes. Watch-out: the census shows 57% pour daily or most days yet average only ~1.55 kegs a month, so the line stays "fresher and cheaper, not more".

**Cashed-up tradies (~20%).** Trade money with a shed, a ute and an unfinished outdoor build. Where 4-tap ($1,950) and 6-tap ($2,550) sales live, and where Afterpay and Zip matter most. Reachable through 4WD and outdoor culture – the Benchy portable tap, Harry's cousin's 4WD fit-out network – and install UGC.

**Baby boomers (~20%).** Established southern-GC households hosting kids and grandkids. Best natural fit for Keg Crew membership ($250/yr [one of two live prices – open item #2], $30 off refills, 5L birthday keg) and the corny-keg service relationship. Slower cycle, lowest creative risk.

**Secondary lenses (~5% each).** DINKs, share houses and status buyers. None warrants its own campaign; each is a variant of an existing lane.

## Overlay 1 – People who already own a system

The cheapest acquisition available, and the one the advisory review ranked first overall.

**116 of 206 owners (56%) arrived this way with no targeted effort at all** – skipping the $975 objection, the partner veto and the three-month cycle, and going straight to the refill margin. No competitor is building this relationship.

What the review added is *how to reach them at scale*: **buy the databases**. Harvey Norman sells six kegerator models plus a 25L tap unit. Keg Land / Kegmaster, BenchTop and Benchy hold buyer lists of their own. Every name on those lists owns a system and has already overcome the objection we spend most of the budget fighting. At about **$70 gross per keg**, the data pays for itself quickly. A direct Harvey Norman arrangement – point-of-sale QR, a referral rebate per keg, or a free keg with purchase where liquor rules allow – sits alongside it.

This overlay is not a segment among segments. It is the first move, and "we fill any system" deserves a permanent campaign lane above every lens in Axis 3.

## Overlay 2 – The partner, catered to rather than managed

The first draft treated the partner as the **approval audience** – the source of the "my wife won't let me" objection, to be answered with "here's what's on tap for her". The review's read is that this is the problem, not the solution:

> "This is a bloke's beer place and the chicks can come if they want. But the blokes will follow the girls."
>
> — advisory session, 27 Aug (recorded segment rather than the typed notes; Clent agreed it is a real blind spot)

Being *covered* is not being *catered for*. Roughly a third of the 27 taps are non-beer – seltzers, a Strawberry Gin Fizz sour, ginger beer, Ranch Water, mojitos on tap – and the RTD range currently reads as a collection of items rather than a proposition. There is no romance in it and the perception internally is that it does not matter. Given the finding above, that is backwards: build the RTD range properly and the rest follows it.

Two further points belong here rather than in a footnote. The unmarketed gluten-reduced and lower-carb range is held ammunition for exactly this audience. And one of the honest unsaid drivers in the category is wanting a reason to be at home but not *in* the house – which cuts against a purely "bring her along" framing and should shape the tone rather than be pretended away.

**The product answer to the veto is a product, not a message.** A collaboration with a barbecue brand – an all-in-one outdoor unit, barbecue plus four taps – sells into the household as *a barbecue*, not a beer rig. That reframes the purchase for the person most likely to block it, which is a harder thing for any campaign to do on its own.

## What this model still cannot tell you

- **Giveaway entries are not demand.** A thousand entries proves a thousand people will enter a free competition. It does not test how many want a system permanently in the backyard. There may be an "occasion" business in the gap – short-term hire for parties – and that is a question, not a plan.
- **The taproom's role in acquisition is unproven.** The 20–30% visitor-to-owner conversion is the client's own estimate and has never been measured (open item #18). It is an extraordinary rate for a $975 purchase. If it is real, the conclusion is to pay to fill the taproom; if it is not, a good deal of the plan changes. Either way it is currently carrying more weight than it can bear.
- **Cost per segment is unknown.** Without COGS, the cost of carrying 27 taps and the taproom's actual monthly loss (open item #17), every ranking here is a judgement about revenue with no view of margin.
