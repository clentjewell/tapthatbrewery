## Purpose

One page the whole engagement can be measured against. If a piece of work does not move one of the four success measures below, it is not strategy – it is activity. Discovery gave us unusually clean success criteria because the founders already think in these terms; our job here is to fix them in writing, define the units precisely, and flag the two numbers that still need founder confirmation.

## The four measures of success

### 1. 1,000 active keg customers (~$2.6M)

The headline number, in the client’s own framing: grow from **206 keg system owners (active subset 96–113 – definition unresolved) to 1,000 active keg system owners or resellers**. At ~2 kegs and ~$200/month average refill spend per active customer, 1,000 actives is a **~$2.6M refill business** – subscription-grade revenue from an asset the customer keeps in their garage.

This is the only growth number that matters, because every other unit exists to feed it (the owner’s stated model: systems → refills; taproom → ownership → refills; wholesale → refills). Keg system sales are *not* a success measure in themselves – hardware is sold at cost ($975 two-tap to $2,550 six-tap) and generates zero margin. A month in which 30 systems sell but active refill customers fall is a bad month.

**"Active" – the precise definition (adopted from the client’s own lifecycle rules):**

| State | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Active** | Keg purchase within the last **75 days** |
| **At-risk** | 75–90 days since last keg purchase |
| **Churned** | 90+ days since last keg purchase |

Every count of "active customers" in this engagement uses the 75-day rule, measured from last keg transaction in GoTab. No softer definition (owns a system, on the mailing list, visited the taproom) counts toward the 1,000.

### 2. Profitability – the P&L out of the red

The business is "always red at the bottom of the P&L" (client’s words). Success is the whole business trading profitably, and the path there is arithmetic, not hope: refills carry the margin, so profitability is substantially a function of measure #1 plus discipline on measure #3. We will treat "consecutive profitable months" as the working indicator once baseline P&L data lands (see open items).

### 3. The taproom judged as a funnel, not a profit centre

The taproom loses money and absorbed the last 18 months of focus. Discovery confirmed its real job: **20–30% of visitors go on to buy a keg system.** Success means the taproom is measured on *conversion* – visitors → demos → giveaway entries → system owners → active refill customers – not on venue P&L alone. A taproom month is good when it manufactures owners, within an agreed loss tolerance (not yet set – see open items). This reframing is itself a success condition: it is what stops taproom gravity from reclaiming founder and marketer attention.

### 4. The franchise horizon as north star

Justin’s stated ambition: **franchise the model on a 10–12 year horizon**, drawing on his Back in Motion franchise background. This does not change what we do in year one, but it changes *how*: every system we build (lifecycle automation, segment campaigns, wholesale playbooks, the switcher offer) should be documented and replicable, because a franchisable business is a business that runs on written systems, not founder heroics. Where two options are otherwise equal, choose the one a franchisee could run.

## What success is not

- Not taproom revenue growth for its own sake – that is the trap the last 18 months already demonstrated.
- Not system sales volume – at-cost hardware is an acquisition cost, not revenue quality.
- Not follower counts or entries – the giveaways (~1,000 entries, 22–25 conversions) matter only as far as they end in active refill customers.
