## Purpose

On 19 August 2026, after the 65-document set was drafted, Tap That Brewery forwarded five documents we had not seen: the customer census (raw tabulations and an analysed write-up), a Scaling Up strategic plan, a growth playbook, and a long-range vision document.

Our set was built from one discovery session and one site visit. It carried the census as open item #1 — the largest blocker to CP1 sign-off. **That blocker is now closed, and closing it invalidates several claims that run through the whole set.**

This document records what changed, what it changed, what the client's own documents disagree about, and what the founders now need to decide. Read it before citing any figure from a draft dated before today.

### One claim we had right — do not "correct" it

Our set says **~50% of refill customers bought their hardware elsewhere.** The census says 31%, and correcting to 31% would have been wrong.

The client's business records show **~90 of 206 systems were sold by Tap That**, meaning **~116 (56%) were bought elsewhere.** The census sample is skewed: engaged customers and Tap-That-origin owners were far likelier to respond. Our ~50% is close to the truth; the survey figure is the outlier.

This is the clearest evidence that the 24% response rate carries real self-selection bias, and it should temper how hard we lean on *every* census percentage.

## Errors in the client's material

Found while reconciling. Two are commercially live.

**1. The "$2.34 a schooner" advertising claim is member-only.** A 20L keg yields ~47 schooners. At $140 that is **$2.98**. $2.34 only holds after the $30 Keg Crew discount. Their own email copy hedges to "$2–$3"; the ad creative does not, and it runs against a "$14 at the pub" comparison. This is an advertising-standards exposure on a live claim and should be fixed before anything else in this document is actioned.

**2. The Keg Crew break-even is wrong at the point of sale.** The playbook tells staff that ~6 kegs a year recovers the $250 membership. It is **8.33** ($250 ÷ $30). Six kegs recovers $180 — customers are being given a number that is $70 short.

**3. Year-1 revenue is roughly double-counted.** $604,800 assumes 180 households live for a full year, but they accrue at 15/month, so the average household is live about six months. Realistic Year 1 is nearer **$300k**; $604,800 is the exit run-rate. Years 2–3 inherit the error, and the model applies no churn against a base that is only 45–55% active.

**4. Figures in the census write-up that the raw data does not support.** Do not publish these: "71% of daily users buy 1–2 kegs" (the cross-tabs give 64%); "three out of four know about Tap Tokens" (it is 92%); "most are comfortable waiting 2–7 days" for delivery (1–2 days is the largest single bucket); "50/50 split" on system origin (it is 44/56 on their own numbers). Also note the write-up's customer avatar — **"Male, 40–55"** — is an assumption: **gender was never asked**, and the age brackets are 41–50 / 51–60.

## What we are revising, and what we are not

**Revising now** — factual corrections that repeat across the set: the customer-count figures, kegs per month and the revenue model built on it, the acquisition-channel ranking, the buying-cycle split, usage frequency, Talos naming, and the franchise horizon.

**Revising with care** — the messaging doctrine. The census inverts our kudos/connection thesis, but three things stop this being a simple flip:

- Kudos is *polarising*, not weak — five respondents ranked it first against connection's one.
- The census write-up's own resolution is a two-stage model: people **buy** on taste, cost and convenience, and **stay** for entertaining and connection. That preserves most of the strategic value of the connection thesis while fixing the acquisition message.
- Freshness may rank first among *existing owners* as a satisfaction driver while being a weak hook for people who have never owned a system. The census and the playbook may be measuring different moments rather than disagreeing.

Notably, **Taste & Freshness — the top-ranked driver — is not one of the client's Four Pillars at all.** Their own write-up jokes that this is "perhaps because we need to find a word that starts with C." That is a real architecture gap, not a joke.

**Not revising without a founder decision** — anything resting on open items 11–15. We will not pick the client's core metric, resolve their lifecycle definition, choose between their three plans, or overturn their deliberate decision not to compete on price. Those are theirs to make, and this document exists to put them in front of them.

## Effect on CP1

CP1 was blocked on the census. The census has arrived and **materially changes the diagnosis** — not its direction, but its numbers and its messaging conclusion. The commercial read in #20 still holds: this is a refill business run with a venue's attention, and the engine is under-fed.

What changes is that the engine is smaller than we said, the customers buy for different reasons than we claimed, and the client already has three strategies for fixing it that do not agree with one another.

**Recommendation:** do not sign CP1 until open items 11–15 are closed. They are questions, not research — a single founders' session should clear them.
