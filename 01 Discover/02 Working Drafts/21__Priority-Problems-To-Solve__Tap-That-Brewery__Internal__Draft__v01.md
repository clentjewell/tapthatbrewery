## How to read this list

These are the bottlenecks D06 named, ranked by commercial consequence, each with a definition of *solved* so progress is checkable rather than felt. The ranking principle: problems that block the refill engine (the margin) outrank problems that merely cost revenue at hospitality margins.

**Not all of these are execution problems.** The business is losing money, nobody has priced what it costs to run, and the taproom and the refill model work against each other by design rather than by drift. Those are structural, and they need a decision rather than effort. P0 below outranks everything that follows it.

## The ranked list

### P0 – Nobody knows what this business costs to run

**The problem.** There is no COGS, no figure for what carrying 27 taps costs in ingredients, kegs, storage and refrigeration on stock that may never move, and no number for the taproom's actual monthly loss. The P&L is described as "red at the bottom" and that is the whole of it.

**Commercial consequence.** This is the largest gap in the engagement, and it sits underneath every other problem here. Without it, every recommendation we have made is a judgement about revenue with no view of margin – including the ranking on this page. A loss-making business cannot fund everything below simultaneously, and we cannot currently say which parts it can fund.

**Solved looks like.** COGS, the cost of the 27-tap range, and the taproom's monthly loss, in writing (open item #17). Test: we can state the break-even active count and the cost per new active, rather than the revenue per new active.

### P1 – Execution capacity (the master problem)

**The problem.** One hybrid marketer (Harry, ~70% marketing / 30% bar cover) is the entire delivery mechanism for segment campaigns, wholesale outreach, re-engagement, UGC, giveaways and events. The segment framework exists on a workshop wall; the campaigns don’t exist at all.

**Commercial consequence.** The $2.6M target has no delivery mechanism. Every other problem on this list persists *because* of this one – at current capacity, fixing P2–P6 means choosing which of them to leave broken.

**Solved looks like.** A brutally sequenced plan (see #22) where Harry’s 70% is spent only on the highest-leverage motions in order; automation absorbing the repeatable work (P2); and an explicit decision at Gate 1 on what will *not* be attempted this year. Test: Harry can say what he is not doing this month, and why, without guilt.

### P2 – Manual re-engagement

**The problem.** Churn management is one hand-sent monthly SMS ($20 off, 2-week window) to customers already 90+ days lapsed. There is no touch at 60 or 75 days, when the customer is still active or merely at-risk. Automation is blocked on the GoTab/Fishbowl integration.

**Commercial consequence.** Every month of delay leaks refill revenue from a list the business already owns – the cheapest revenue available anywhere in the plan. Churned customers contacted personally said they had lapsed, not left ("hadn’t gotten around to coming in"), which means this is recoverable revenue dying of inattention.

**Solved looks like.** Lifecycle automation live on interval-based triggers keyed to each customer's own reorder rhythm (#48), with the flat 60/75/90 rails as the fallback for thin histories (OKR 2.1); an at-risk save rate ≥ 40% (OKR 2.2); the monthly SMS retired as a system, retained only as a tested offer within the ladder.

### P3 – Taproom gravity

**The problem.** 18 months of drift pulled founder and marketer attention into the loss-making venue because it is visible, social, and always urgent. The unit that was meant to be the shopfront became the main character.

**Commercial consequence.** Attention is the scarcest resource in the business (see P1); the taproom consumes it at hospitality returns while the refill engine – the actual margin – sits at 96–113 of a 1,000 target. Unchecked, the venue will keep winning attention by default.

**This is not only an attention problem.** It is a **zero-sum** one: the refill business is built on people *not* coming in, so filling the room fills the half that loses money. And the conversion rate that justified calling it a funnel – 20–30% – is the client's own estimate and has never been measured (open item #18). At that rate the rational move would be to pay people to walk in, which is itself grounds for doubt.

**Solved looks like.** A founders' decision on what the taproom is for (open item #20): restricted hours as a tasting and event venue, or an open drop-in that is knowingly paid for. Then, whichever way it goes, a measured conversion rate rather than an estimate, and an agreed loss tolerance. Test: the monthly review discusses taproom *cost and conversion* before taproom *takings* – and somebody can state both numbers.

### P4 – Wholesale lead leakage

**The problem.** DM outreach works – venues respond and pass on manager contacts – but follow-up is admittedly loose. Leads die between first reply and manager contact. There is no log, no SLA, no pipeline.

**Commercial consequence.** The one wholesale motion that demonstrably generates warm leads is being wasted at the cheapest stage of the funnel. Every dead lead is lost refill volume (wholesale exists to feed refills) plus a burned first impression in a small local market.

**Solved looks like.** Every lead logged, first follow-up within 48 hours, style-matched pitch collateral ready to send (Japanese lager → Japanese restaurants etc.), and 6 new venue accounts in 12 months (OKR 3.4). Test: zero leads without a next-action date.

### P5 – Price and collateral drift

**The problem.** Two live membership posters with conflicting prices and token values ($250/yr vs $300 + $120/yr renewal; 100 vs differing bonus tokens); the tasting paddle is $18 on the sign and $20 on the screen.

**Commercial consequence.** Small in dollars, corrosive in trust: the brand’s premium-over-competitors position ($120–170 kegs vs $85–90 across the road) rests on "consistent quality," and inconsistent pricing undercuts it at the till. Worse, it **contaminates every downstream asset** – no funnel page, membership pitch, or campaign copy can be built until one price is true.

**Solved looks like.** One reconciled price list confirmed by the founders (a named Gate 1 production gate), old collateral physically removed, and a single source of truth for pricing that all future collateral cites. Test: any two customer-facing price mentions agree.

### P6 – Food friction caps functions

**The problem.** No kitchen or food licence. Private bookings – a proven earner on the minimum-spend model (1 drink/person/hour; missed only once) – are patched with Ammazza pizza and snack packs; food trucks demand guaranteed numbers and are hard to secure.

**Commercial consequence.** Function revenue growth is gated, and functions are one of the taproom’s best funnel moments (a room of 40–130 prospects watching the tap wall work). The cap costs both venue revenue and system-buyer exposure.

**Solved looks like.** A systemic answer, not per-event scrambling: a standing food-truck roster or catering partner arrangement that a booking can be sold against. Ranked last because it improves the funnel’s capacity rather than the engine itself – solve after P1–P4 are moving.
