## Purpose of this pack

Pulls the discovery session, the site visit, the client's records and the advisory review into one validated read. This is **not** the final strategy. It is the diagnosis the Design phase is built on, and it separates what is confirmed from what is still assumption throughout.

## Summary on a page

**Business snapshot.** Tap That Brewery, Burleigh Heads. Founded by Chris Smith (head brewer, ex Burleigh Barrels) and Justin Mistry (physio turned operator). Opened October; won Brewer's Choice at Crafted in its first year with Midnight in Tokyo. Four units: keg system sales, keg refills, the taproom, wholesale. Twenty-seven taps, about a third deliberately non-beer. **206 keg system owners, 96–113 of them active**, against a target of 1,000. Currently loss-making.

**Core commercial read.** *Tap That is a refill subscription business that has been run as a brewery with a bar — and the two halves cancel each other out. The refill model needs people to stay home. The taproom needs them to come in. Nobody has costed either side.*

**Immediate opportunity.** 116 of 206 owners bought their hardware somewhere else and came across with no targeted effort at all. The cheapest growth available is not creating new system owners — it is **buying the lists of people who already own one**.

**The single largest gap.** There is no COGS, no figure for what carrying 27 taps costs, and no number for the taproom's monthly loss. Until those land, every recommendation in this engagement is a judgement about revenue with no view of margin, and this pack says so rather than implying otherwise.

## Executive summary

The problem is not awareness, demand, or offer quality. The product is award-winning in its first year, the model is sound on paper, and the owner's own mental model is correct: everything feeds refill volume.

**The problem is that the business runs two models that work against each other, and has never priced either.** Refills are a subscription business whose customers succeed by staying at home. The taproom is a hospitality business whose customers succeed by coming in. For eighteen months the attention went to the taproom, because a venue is visible, social and always urgent — and it is the half that loses money. The refill engine, which carries all the margin, sits at 96–113 active of a targeted 1,000.

That is not only an allocation problem, which is how the first version of this pack read it. It is a **shape** problem, and it resolves with a founders' decision about what the taproom is for, not with a better content calendar.

Underneath it sits a second problem that makes the first hard to solve honestly: **nobody knows what this business costs to run.** Twenty-seven taps means ingredients, kegs, storage and refrigeration on stock that may never move. The P&L is described as "red at the bottom" and that is the whole of it. A loss-making business cannot attempt everything, and without cost data we cannot say which parts it can afford.

## What we heard, validated, in the client's terms

- *"We're in the business of connection and good times — keg systems and refills are the means, not the end."*
- Systems are sold **at cost** ($975 for two taps). All margin lives in refills, at roughly **$70 gross per keg** and about $155–200 a month per active customer.
- *"Always red at the bottom of the P&L."* The venue trades, and the business loses money overall.
- 20L kegs against the industry's 50L: fresher, more variety, no excise under the threshold, no wholesaler margin — about $275 equivalent against $500 for a 50L through a wholesaler.
- About 70% of corny keg customers have moved to Talos kegs: no CO2, pick up and go.
- The buying cycle is long. Around 60% take up to three months, 40% longer, about 10% inside a month.
- **The census (n=49):** 57% pour daily or most days, yet average only ~1.55 kegs a month. Moderation, not excess.
- **Refill cadence splits three ways:** roughly 10% buy monthly, 60% about every three months, 40% run longer.
- Lifetime gross profit: about **$2,100** residential against **$21,840** for a venue account.

**What the client's own census document asked, before we did.** The raw tabulation opens with three questions from the team: *"How valid is this sample size? Do we need to factor in any biases — respondents are likely 'engaged' customers? Does this give a true representation of ALL keg system owners?"* They were right to ask. The advisory review reached the same conclusion independently.

## Business and operating model

| Unit | Role in the plan | Economics |
|---|---|---|
| **Keg refills** | The margin engine. Everything is judged by its effect here | ~$70 gross per keg; ~$155–200/month per active; 206 owners, 96–113 active |
| **Keg systems** | Acquisition offer, deliberately margin-free | $975 (2-tap) to $2,550 (6-tap); mini 5L $225; integrated from $1,750; Afterpay and Zip |

| **Commercial & wholesale** | **Promoted.** One account is worth roughly ten households | Venue LTGP ~$21,840 against ~$2,100 residential; one event drives a week of taproom trade; needs an owner, goals and KPIs |

Lifecycle definitions in use: **active** under 75 days since last keg, **at-risk** 75–90, **churned** 90+ — though which definition is live is itself unresolved (45, 75 or 90 days appear in three different client documents, and it decides whether the active base is 96 or 113).

## Audience synthesis

The model has three axes, and the demographic lenses are the least important of them.

**1. Refill cadence — the margin axis.** 10% monthly (at ceiling, protect), 60% quarterly (the core), 40% longer. The 40% is where the growth is: they have **cut back rather than defected**, and the system is still in the backyard. Where they are also members, they are paying for something they have stopped using.

**2. Commercial and wholesale — the leverage axis.** One account worth ten households. Venue trade, events, sports clubs on a minimum-guarantee model, and the tour operators who let the customer pick which breweries to visit.

**3. Household lenses — the creative axis.** Young families (~45%), high-volume drinkers (~20%), cashed-up tradies (~20%), baby boomers (~20%), then share houses, DINKs and status buyers. Client estimates, deliberately overlapping, and not summing to 100%.

**Behavioural overlay, ranked above all three lenses:** people who already own a system. 116 of 206 arrived this way with no effort, skipping the $975 objection, the partner veto and the three-month cycle.

**On the drivers.** The census ranks them 1 Taste & Freshness **2.19**, 2 Cost **2.75**, 3 Convenience **2.81**, 4 Connection **3.61**, 5 Kudos **3.64**. Read that as **the order people will state**, not the order that moves them. Forty-nine self-selected respondents will not write down that they want to be seen as more successful than their mates. Ego, status and wanting a reason to be at home without being *in* the house are real and unsayable. The workshop's original read — cost as the dirty little secret, status doing the buying — is not superseded by the census so much as unreachable by it.

**The partner is an audience, not a gate.** The first read treated the partner as the source of the *"my wife won't let me"* objection, answered with "here's what's on tap for her". The review's read is that the framing is the problem: women are **covered rather than catered for**, the RTD range is an afterthought with no romance in it, and the blokes follow the girls. The product answer beats the message answer — a barbecue-brand collaboration sells into the household as a barbecue, not a beer rig.

## Market and white space

Local rivals (Aardvark and Arrow, Laughing Lizard) and online distributors (Kegs Off Tap, Keg Republic) sell kegs. Nobody owns the **category** — the installed base of keg systems sitting in Gold Coast garages that need filling every month.

> **The white space, quotable:** *Nobody owns "beer on tap at home" on the Gold Coast. Tap That can be the brand every keg system owner refills with, whoever sold them the hardware — and the way to own it is to buy the lists of people who already own one.*

**The nearest fight is weaker than assumed.** Aardvark and Arrow's domain is suspended and they have joined with Burleigh Homebrew. The "cheaper across the road" threat that shapes several of our arguments may not be live.

**But the position is not.** Tap That currently sits dearer than the keg rivals and short of a genuine premium — the middle ground. The review's counter-example is Second Earth Brewery, who sat in exactly that space until an investor told them so, moved a dark beer into 500ml cans at about $50 for four, and now cannot keep up. **Premium beats the middle, and the middle is a position reached by not deciding.**

Supporting wedge: the **Benchy** portable tap (4WD, camping, boating, caravan) has no direct competitor.

## Offer synthesis

| Tier | Offer | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Entry** | Taproom visit, tasting paddle, 5L mini system ($225), party hire | Low-risk first taste |
| **Spine** | 2-tap system $975 at cost (+$40 bonus credit) → 20L refills | The conversion moment. Hardware at cost is the *offer*; refills are the *revenue* |
| **LTV engine** | Keg Crew membership ($250/yr — one of two live prices, open item #2), $30 off refills, Tap Tokens, referral rewards | Locks the cadence; makes churn visible |
| **Expansion** | Benchy portable, delivery via GoTab × Uber Direct, lease-to-buy, commercial and white-label | New pools of refill demand |

**The model is razor-and-blades in reverse** — an expensive handle and cheap blades — which is why every barrier traces back to the $975. The answer is to remove the wall rather than argue with it: rental or lease-to-buy priced like a **gym membership at $50–100 a month**. Free delivery as a member benefit, or $150 non-member and $130 member delivered, shares the cost and puts Justin in front of customers instead of behind the bar.

**Simplify the loyalty layer before automating it.** A points system carries administration costs of its own. Every tenth keg free is legible and needs almost no administration.

**The re-education pathway exists in the venue and nowhere else.** Taproom → demo → giveaway → 50% close is real; the three-month buying cycle is still nurtured by memory.

## Bottlenecks and risks, each with its commercial consequence

1. **Nobody knows what the business costs to run.** No COGS, no cost of carrying 27 taps, no taproom loss figure. Every recommendation, including this pack's, is a revenue judgement with no margin view. *This outranks everything below it.*
2. **The two halves cancel out.** Filling the taproom fills the loss-making side of a business whose margin depends on people staying home. Unresolved, every campaign is partly self-defeating.
3. **Execution capacity.** One marketer at ~70% cannot run segment campaigns, commercial outreach, automation, UGC and events. The $2.6M target has no delivery mechanism.
4. **Manual re-engagement, badly timed.** Churn management is a hand-sent monthly SMS. A blanket 90-day clock is a month late for the 10% who buy monthly and premature for the 40% who run longest.
5. **Commercial left unowned.** The channel worth ten households per account has no structure, goals, KPIs or person. Cold walk-ins failed, which is a finding about method, not about the opportunity.
6. **Price and collateral drift.** Two live membership posters with different prices. Three sets of schooner maths in circulation, with a live ad running on one of them.
7. **Food friction caps functions.** No kitchen or licence limits private bookings, gating function revenue.

## Strategic hypotheses, to test in Design

Proposed, not decided.

- **H1 — Bought lists beat built audiences.** Buying the Harvey Norman, Keg Land, BenchTop and Benchy buyer lists acquires refill customers more cheaply than any campaign, because the $975 objection is already overcome.
- **H2 — Commercial outperforms consumer per unit of effort**, on the ten-households ratio, and needs a person rather than a channel plan.
- **H3 — The 40% who have slowed need a product, not a message.** A low-alcohol or healthier line reactivates owners who have cut back on beer, not on thirst.
- **H4 — Reorder timing beats reorder discounting.** Contact keyed to each customer's own interval outperforms a blanket clock and a post-churn discount.
- **H5 — Premium beats the middle.** Moving up resolves the position; matching the rivals' price does not.
- **H6 — The taproom is worth less than assumed as a funnel and more than assumed as a venue** — a tasting and event room, not a drop-in.
- **H7 — The website's problem is hierarchy, not structure.** Four co-equal business units and a buried award is a positioning failure expressed as a layout.
