**Purpose.** The full-funnel view of how a stranger becomes a repeat refill customer, with every known conversion number placed, every unmeasured point marked, and the leaks ranked. The context that disciplines everything below: the target is **1,000 active keg customers from today’s 96–113**, and the best-performing sales event in the business currently adds **22–25 owners twice a year** – at that rate alone, the gap takes 15+ years to close. The funnel must leak less and the switcher lane must open.

## The tension this funnel does not draw

Before the stages: **the taproom and the refill model are zero-sum.** The refill business is built on people *not* coming in, and the taproom exists to bring them in. Drawn as a single funnel, stage 2a looks like a feeder into stage 3. In the accounts it is also the half of the business that loses money, so every visitor acquired there is acquired at a cost this funnel does not show. The diagram is not wrong; it is incomplete, and it flatters the taproom by omission.

That is why the taproom's role is a founders' decision (open item #20) rather than a funnel-optimisation question.

**The parallel funnel with no top:** **116 of 206 owners (56%) are switchers** – they bought hardware elsewhere and arrived organically. This is stage 4 entry without stages 1–3's cost, and it currently has no campaign, no offer and no landing page.

The top this funnel is missing is **buying the lists**. Harvey Norman sells six kegerator models plus a 25L tap unit; Keg Land / Kegmaster, BenchTop and Benchy hold buyer lists of their own. Every name owns a system and has already overcome the $975 objection that stage 3 exists to fight. At about $70 gross per keg, the data pays for itself quickly. This is now ranked the first move in the engagement (#22, #24 P1).

**Commercial as a feeder, and it is bigger than this funnel implies:** DMs → manager contact → (leak) → tap placement → venue refill volume. Best-responding channel, follow-up admitted loose. On the arithmetic **one commercial account is worth roughly ten households** – venue lifetime gross profit ~$21,840 against ~$2,100 residential – and one event drives a week of taproom trade. Sports clubs are gated by cans, contracts, taps and CUB exclusivity as a cold channel, but work on a minimum-guarantee model (free system against X kegs a month across the season). Tour operators sit alongside: Hop On, Urban Legends and Pineapple Tours let the customer pick the breweries, so the write-up is the conversion asset.

## What the numbers say

- **The demo may work.** The 20–30% visitor-to-owner conversion is the client's own estimate and has never been measured (#20B). It is an extraordinary claim for a $975+ purchase – even if validation halves it, the taproom is the best sales asset in the business. Its problem is anonymity: the 70–80% who leave without buying are not captured, and ~60% of eventual buyers take up to 3 months to decide.
- **The giveaway is a list machine being used as a lottery.** ~2,000 self-qualified leads per year; ~45–50 convert on the single 50%-off touch; the rest receive nothing until the next competition. A structured 90-day nurture matched to the known objection set is hypothesis H3, and it is the highest-leverage test in the funnel.
- **The engine measures itself, the funnel doesn’t.** Stages 5 and 7 have crisp lifecycle definitions (75/90 days) because refills run through the till; everything upstream runs on estimate and recall. GoTab/Fishbowl integration is the instrumentation dependency for nearly every ⚠ above.

## The three biggest leaks (ranked)

1. **Post-giveaway nurture.** ~975 warm entrants per cycle, contact details in hand, one offer, then silence – against a buying cycle where 60% need up to 3 months. This is the largest identified pool of near-buyers leaking today.
2. **The at-risk window (75–90 days).** Nothing touches a customer between "active" and "churned"; the first intervention is a $20 discount after the relationship has already lapsed. Churn interviews say these customers are lapsed, not lost – a pre-emptive nudge timed to their own reorder interval (H4, #48) defends ~$200/month per save.
3. **The 40% who have slowed down.** Roughly four in ten customers run longer than three months between refills. They have **cut back rather than defected** – the system is still in the backyard, and where they are members they are paying for something they have stopped using. The fix is a product rather than a message: a low-alcohol or healthier line, framed as hydration, reactivates them without asking anyone to drink more. This is the largest pool in the base and it currently has no lane at all.

4. **Commercial follow-up.** The only outreach channel that produced responses (DMs) leaked its leads between first reply and manager contact. Pipeline discipline, not marketing, is the fix – and on the ten-households ratio each converted venue compounds harder than the table above suggests.

Close behind: taproom lead capture (leak 1's twin – same fix: capture + nurture), and the unbuilt switcher lane (not a leak but an unopened tap).

## Assumptions & open items

- Taproom conversion (20–30%) and giveaway figures are client estimate/recall – validate against POS and entry lists.
- No data yet on: taproom traffic, giveaway list size retained (consent), refill activation rate, membership attach rate, winback response, referral volume. Instrumentation plan belongs in Design (DS02/DS03), gated on GoTab/Fishbowl.
- Membership economics used here assume the newer poster ($250/yr, $30/refill) – two prices are live and unreconciled (open item #2).
- The 10/60/40 cadence split is client observation, not counted refills. Square holds the real distribution (open item #12), and until it lands the stage-5 average is doing work it cannot really carry.
- This funnel measures revenue paths only. What each stage *costs* is unknown (open item #17), so "biggest leak" here means largest volume, not largest margin.
