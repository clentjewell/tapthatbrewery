Where the money actually is

The system is the barrier. The refill is the business. Attention has been going to neither.

Systems go out close to cost, and the margin arrives later at roughly $70 gross a keg. That makes this a subscription business wearing a brewery's clothes. A subscription business needs people to stay home and reorder. The Tap Room needs them to come in. Eighteen months of attention went to the half that loses money.

The fix is not optimisation, it is reshaping. Running a brewery and a hospitality venue on a skeleton staff is not a resourcing problem to be solved with more hours.

Justin frames the direction question as three-way: a Taproom selling kegs, a Showroom selling beers, or an Event space doing both. JP's recommendation is the second or the third, with walk-in paused. Justin's own priority list keeps taproom trade alive, growing it from local business, functions and ticketed music.

The cheapest growth is still the 116 who already own a system and already refill here. Their objections are overcome. Nobody has to be sold on the idea of beer at home.

Partners move this number, campaigns do not. Harvey Norman, Kegland and Benchy each put Tap That in front of people who have already bought the hardware. Justin's one-year marker is cashflow positive, with JM taking a wage.

The climb to 1,000 is not a marketing target, it is a distribution target. It needs partners, not posts.

Unit economics, and the argument

One wholesale account is worth about ten households. That ratio is the whole case for resourcing wholesale ahead of another consumer campaign.

Members leak at 18%, non-members at 44%. Attaching membership to a system sale is the single highest-return retention move available.

$2.55 a schooner at home, 2.5 cases for the price of one. Against $12+ at the pub and $80+ for a case at the bottleshop.

63% of customers said they would host a demo at their own place. That is a distribution channel sitting unused, and it costs nothing but coordination.

Two membership programmes are running against one business. If the room becomes a destination and refills lead, one of them is paying for a shape the business no longer has. Redesign both around the refill, or drop one. Justin lists a taproom membership as a three-month item, so the tension is live.

The open item is no longer which maths, it is making sure the live ad and every piece of collateral carry $2.55.

The canning question

Christy raised it. Tap That pushed back. It is not in Justin's plan.

For: a bottle shop is where people find out the brand exists, which no keg marketing reaches. It also opens bars and restaurants that will not give up a tap.

Against: low margin, high effort, and it competes directly with the refill model this plan is built on. Tap That has already priced a small line and parked it.

What would settle it: a costed run through the cannery contact next door, priced against Earth Brew at about $36 a four-pack.

Worth revisiting if walk-in trade is wound back and a new awareness channel is needed.

What could go wrong
  • Pausing walk-ins costs community. The room built the ambassadors. Replace it with systems before it goes, not after.
  • Wholesale hire without a process. A salesperson with no CRM behind them will fail the same way the founders did.
  • Chris is trapped in production. Justin's words: highly valued and intelligent, but the business needs his time and knowledge freed up.
  • Everything at once. The recurring failure has been starting things and moving on. Three moves finished beats ten started.

Justin's own point in the catch-up: as this scales, the personal connection cannot hold. It has to be replicated in systems.
