The read

A refill subscription business is being run with a hospitality venue’s attention – and it is losing money.

Systems go out near cost. The margin is the refill, at about $70 gross a keg. But eighteen months of attention went to the taproom, which loses money and was only ever meant to be the shopfront – and the two halves work against each other. The refill business is built on people not coming in. Every campaign that fills the taproom fills the losing half.

Written after a 27 August advisory review found the original pack accurate but not hard enough. This sheet is the harder version.

The Core : the shape of the business
Customers

116 of 206 owners bought their system somewhere else and refill here anyway. That is the cheapest growth available and it is flagged in no system.

The 40% who buy less than quarterly are where the growth is – not the monthly buyers, who cannot drink much more. Many are members who simply cut back.

One commercial account is worth about ten households, and one event drives a week of taproom trade. Both facts sat in the pack without ever being the headline.

63% would host a demo at their own place. No programme exists to take them up on it.

65% are aged 41–60. A volume model is running against an ageing, moderating demographic.

Offer

Reverse razor-and-blades: expensive handle, cheap blades. The system is the barrier; the refill is the business.

Fix the barrier – lease-to-buy at $50–100 a month, framed like a gym membership. Cut the range to six core beers plus seasonals; 27 taps is decision anxiety for the customer and unpriced cost for the business.

Remove the friction. Free delivery as a member benefit, or $150 non-member and $130 member delivered. It also puts Justin in front of customers instead of behind the bar.

A low-alcohol line is a product, not a trend note. Lapsed members still own the system and still need to drink something.

Sell the schooner, not the keg. Settle which figure first – three are in circulation.

Rivals

The enemy is the pub and the bottle shop, not the operator across the road.

Aardvark and Arrow is cheaper at $85–95, but their domain is suspended and they have had to merge with Burleigh Homebrew. Competing on their price is a fight not worth having.

The real rival is doing nothing – the keg that sits. Non-members leak at 44% against 18% for members, and that is drift rather than defection.

Being dearer is defensible if the story is unit economics against a $10–18 pub schooner. It is indefensible as a sticker fight.

Nobody on the Gold Coast owns this category, in market or in search.

Expression

The voice on the walls is already right. It is nowhere else.

Chris is invisible. An award-winning head brewer who has never explained the tasting notes to a Friday regular. The website gives four business units equal weight and buries both the award and “fits any system”.

The taproom has no X factor. Shuffleboard is not one. An industrial park will not win foot traffic on atmosphere alone.

Referral is 18% of acquisition and only 43.8% of the base knows the keg-system reward exists.

Open: name versus mark, and no vector artwork exists.

The three moves : ranked, 27 Aug
  1. Buy the system-owner databases. Harvey Norman, Keg Land / Kegmaster, BenchTop. Every name already owns a system, so the $975 objection is pre-cleared. At ~$70 a keg the data pays for itself.
  2. Do a deal with Harvey Norman directly. POS QR, referral rebate per keg, or a free keg with purchase. A conversation, not a campaign.
  3. Tour operators, and wholesale properly. The operator picks the breweries, so the write-up decides it – and ours omits the award. Wholesale needs structure, KPIs and a person.

Ranked below all three: pulling people into the taproom through social media.

Six decisions only you can make
  • The taproom – tasting and event venue, or keep paying for a drop-in.
  • The range – six core beers, or 27 taps.
  • Membership price – two posters, two prices, both live in the venue.
  • “Active” – 45, 75 or 90 days. It decides every number above.
  • Which plan is live – three documents name three core metrics.
  • Model A or B – sell more systems, or convert the 116 who bought elsewhere.
What we got wrong
Said plainly, because it changes what you should trust.
  • We never wrote down that the business is losing money.
  • We treated the taproom as a funnel when it is also a cost.
  • We leaned on a 20–30% conversion the business has never measured.
  • We anchored on a 50-person census that cannot carry a strategy alone.
  • We called Harry “Raef” in 160 places.
