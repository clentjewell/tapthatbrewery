The one thing everything is built on05 / 12

The read.

If the sheets in section four are the shape of it, this is the argument underneath. The whole thing in one sentence – and it is harder than the one we gave you a week ago.

A refill subscription business is being run with a hospitality venue’s attention – and it is losing money.

The engine

Systems go out near cost. The margin is the refill – about $70 gross a keg, at roughly 1.55 kegs a month per customer. 206 systems in the database and 96–113 actives, because your own documents define “active” three different ways.

The zero-sum problem

The refill business is built on people not coming in. So every campaign that fills the taproom fills the half that loses money. We had the taproom down as a funnel. It is at least as much a cost, and nobody has priced what 27 taps costs to carry.

The cheapest growth

Roughly 116 of your 206 owners – 56% – bought their system somewhere else and came to you for beer anyway, with no campaign at all. Nothing in your systems flags who they are, and every one of them has already cleared the $975 objection.

Everything downstream follows from that: the installed base before new owners, the databases before the advertising, one commercial account ahead of ten households, and the taproom judged on what it feeds rather than what it takes.

One thing the census changed outright. We had written cost as the closer – the thing you justify with once kudos and connection have done the selling. Your customers rank it second, at 2.75, just behind taste and freshness at 2.19. So taste leads and cost states itself plainly. The caveat now sits on the census itself: fifty self-selected responses cannot carry a strategy on their own, and nobody names status or ego in a survey.

Before anything else07 / 12

What we got wrong.

Said plainly, because it changes what you should trust in the rest of this. All of it came out of the 27 August review, and all of it is corrected in the documents rather than noted and left.

01
We never wrote down that the business is losing money.
Not once, across seventy-nine documents. Everything we handed you was a marketing plan for a business that cannot presently fund one, and not saying so let us recommend things you are not in a position to do.
02
We treated the taproom as a funnel when it is also a cost.
The refill business is built on people not coming in. We had campaigns driving traffic into the half of the business that loses money, and we never named the tension.
03
We leaned on a conversion rate nobody has measured.
The taproom converting 20–30% of visitors to refill customers runs through the set as a proof point. It is your estimate, never observed. At that rate the right answer would be to pay people to walk in. It is now flagged in the thirty documents that use it.
04
We anchored too hard on the census.
Fifty self-selected people who do not necessarily know, or admit, why they buy. It stays as a strong secondary input – your business records still outrank it, and the 56% switcher figure stands – but it is not the anchor, and status and ego will never show up in a self-report.
05
We called Harry “Raef” in a hundred and sixty places.
Across forty-eight documents, and we had conflated him with someone on our side. Corrected everywhere. It is the smallest error here and the one you would have noticed first, which is rather the point.
And what we still have not done
The website
Strategy, sitemap, page strategy and build brief are written. The site is not designed or built, and we have not audited what is live.
Brand artwork
Scoped as import-and-lock, not redesign. No new identity has been designed and none should be without you asking.
Your ad account
A previous agency ran Meta campaigns and we have not been inside the account. What worked and what did not is still unrecovered.
The cost side
No COGS, no P&L, no measured cost of carrying 27 taps. This is the gap that matters most, and it is why the next step is a conversation rather than another document.
If you do three things08 / 12

The three moves.

Christy was asked which three, out of everything raised, we would actually put our name to. These are they. The ranking is deliberate, and what sits below them matters less than it looks.

01
Buy the databases of people who already own a system.
Harvey Norman lists six kegerator models plus a 25L tap unit. Keg Land, Kegmaster and BenchTop hold buyer lists of their own. Every name on them owns hardware, which means the $975 objection – the single most expensive thing your marketing fights – is already cleared. At about $70 gross a keg, the data pays for itself quickly. Nothing in the original plan pursued this.
Owner: Justin + Jewell · The cheapest acquisition available to you
02
Do a deal with Harvey Norman directly.
A QR code at the point of sale, a referral rebate on each keg, or a free keg with purchase if the liquor rules allow it. Monk is one of their closest allies and holds point contacts. This is a conversation, not a campaign, and it is worth having before any budget moves.
Owner: Justin · Warm introduction available
03
Go at the tour operators, and run wholesale properly.
Hop On, Urban Legends and Pineapple Tours all let the customer choose which breweries to visit – so the write-up decides whether you get picked, and yours does not mention Midnight in Tokyo while Balter gets the imagery. Send them fresh copy, photos, reviews and the award. On wholesale: one commercial account is worth about ten households, and it currently has no structure, no KPIs and nobody carrying it.
Owner: Harry (tours) + Justin (wholesale)
Ranked below all three

Pulling people into the taproom through social media. That was close to the centre of the activation plan we wrote, and on this read it should not be. If the taproom is the half that loses money, spending the marketing budget filling it is the wrong instinct however good the content is.
